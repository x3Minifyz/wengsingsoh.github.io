From the Big Four to Industry: What Changed, and What I'd Tell My Younger Self

Five years in audit taught me how to examine a business. Moving into industry taught me how to understand one from the inside.

SWS Soh Weng Sing
Nov 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  1. Technical skill gets you in; human connections help you get things done.
  2. Real businesses rarely follow the textbook. Finance is about navigating trade-offs with discipline.
  3. Keep asking why. Finance should help shape what happens next, not only report what happened.

The dream of joining the Big Four

For many accounting and finance graduates, joining the Big Four — Deloitte, EY, KPMG and PwC — is a career milestone in itself, much as McKinsey, BCG and Bain are for aspiring consultants.

These firms offer global recognition, exposure to a wide range of industries, structured development and the chance to work alongside some incredibly talented people. For many of us, getting in was once a dream, and it came with a sense of prestige, as if it would instantly boost our confidence, knowledge and professional standing.

I was no different.

I joined KPMG in January 2022 with a mixture of ambition, curiosity and the desire to challenge myself. I wanted to build a strong technical foundation, gain exposure to different businesses and develop into a well-rounded finance professional.

But getting in is one thing. Surviving and progressing is another.

Five years in audit: learning, growing and occasionally complaining

My first four months at KPMG were particularly challenging.

I was assigned to a new first-year audit engagement, and there wasn't much upfront preparation or a ready-made roadmap for us to follow. We practically started from scratch and worked our way towards completing the engagement.

It was overwhelming, especially being relatively new to the profession. There were plenty of things to figure out, problems to resolve and deadlines to meet. But looking back, it was also an incredibly rewarding experience. The sense of accomplishment from taking something from the ground up and seeing it through to completion was significant. That experience also contributed to me receiving a promotion within my first six months at the firm.

As the years went by, I was exposed to multiple complex listed companies and gradually took on greater responsibilities. I moved from completing assigned audit work to managing engagements, coordinating teams and dealing with more complicated accounting and business matters.

One experience that stood out was an M&A project I led alongside my audit responsibilities. Rather than focusing purely on historical financial information, I had the opportunity to examine the potential benefits of an acquisition, assess the target's post-acquisition opportunities, develop recommendations and solutions, and prepare a presentation deck to communicate the findings.

It was a different experience from traditional audit work, and one that challenged me to think beyond accounting. Being recognised as a high performer that year was certainly encouraging.

Of course, not every day was as exciting as working on an M&A project.

One interesting thing about audit is the exposure it gives you to businesses across many industries. One engagement might be in healthcare, another in manufacturing, retail or technology. Every company has its own business model, operating environment and challenges.

Yet, despite all these differences, there was one thing that often remained surprisingly consistent: the working hours.

We were accustomed to working until 10pm, sometimes even later, with the occasional night stretching until 2 or 3am. By then, we would book a Grab home (thankfully, paid for by the company!), get home, take a shower and, before finally calling it a night, check our work phones one last time to see whether our managers or partners had sent any messages.

Set the alarm, hopefully get some sleep, wake up at 9am and repeat the cycle.

(Well, waking up at 9am was actually one of the perks of my team's culture. If we had worked until midnight or later, we were generally allowed to head into the office a little later the following morning!)

My colleagues and I would complain about almost everything along the way. The workload, clients with difficult attitudes, late submission of PBC (Prepared by Client) documents that left us chasing deadlines, and those seemingly endless review notes from managers that sometimes felt longer than the audit working papers themselves.

Looking back, there were certainly moments when we questioned why we were putting ourselves through all of this.

But with time, I realised that these experiences were also part of the learning process.

If there was one thing KPMG taught me that I would carry throughout my career, it would be the importance of human connections.

In school, we are taught accounting standards, financial reporting, audit methodologies and technical knowledge. But professional life is rarely that straightforward.

You work with people from different backgrounds, personalities, experiences and expectations. You have to understand how to communicate with your reporting managers, support your subordinates, collaborate with your peers, lead engagement teams and manage relationships with external clients.

Everyone thinks and works in their own way. What works with one individual may not work with another.

You gradually realise that being technically correct does not automatically mean you can get things done. Sometimes, the challenge is not identifying the problem, but getting everyone involved to understand it, align with one another and work towards a solution.

That is a skill that no textbook can fully teach.

The curiosity that eventually took me beyond audit

Leaving audit was not a decision that came overnight. It had been on my mind for quite some time.

I was always curious about how other companies operated, particularly those in commercial and corporate environments.

What does their working life actually look like? Are they just as busy as we are? What kind of challenges do they face? Are their problems fundamentally different from ours, given that we all came from the same accounting and finance background?

I had spent years looking at businesses from the perspective of an external auditor. I understood how to examine financial statements, identify risks, challenge accounting treatments and ensure that financial records were fairly presented.

But I wanted to understand what happened behind those numbers.

How were operational decisions made? Why did management choose one direction over another? How did companies balance profitability, growth, manpower, operational efficiency and internal controls?

More importantly, I wanted to move beyond identifying what had happened and become more involved in determining what could happen next.

That was what eventually brought me into industry.

From reviewing businesses to being part of one

Moving into industry gave me a different perspective on the role of finance.

One of the biggest realisations was that businesses do not operate in a perfect textbook environment.

In theory, processes should be efficient, controls should be effective, information should be complete and decisions should always be rational.

In reality, businesses are run by people. People have different priorities, limitations, motivations and sometimes conflicting interests. There are resource constraints, operational inefficiencies, competing commercial objectives and, occasionally, human greed or simply the tendency to take the easier route.

There is rarely one perfect solution that fits every situation.

The challenge is learning how to navigate these realities, establish the right balance and find practical ways to improve the business without losing sight of financial discipline and good governance.

My experience in group finance has also broadened my understanding of what finance can contribute. Whether it is group-level reporting, supporting acquisition-related activities, improving financial processes or participating in the implementation of a new ERP system, the work increasingly involves understanding how different parts of the business come together.

A system implementation, for example, is not simply about getting the accounting software to work. It involves understanding existing processes, identifying gaps, aligning different departments and getting people to adapt to a new way of working.

These experiences have made me appreciate that finance is not just about accounting for transactions or ensuring that yesterday's numbers are correct.

It is about understanding why things happen, what the numbers tell us and how we can use that information to help the business move forward.

What I would tell my younger self

If I could go back to my first day at KPMG, I would probably tell myself a few things.

First, do not underestimate the value of the people you meet. Technical knowledge can be learned, but the ability to build relationships, communicate effectively and earn trust takes time and experience.

Second, do not measure your career solely by promotions, job titles or the prestige of the organisation you work for. These things matter, but they do not necessarily define how much you have grown professionally.

Third, do not be afraid to step outside your comfort zone. The skills that made you successful in your previous role may not be sufficient for the next one. Be willing to unlearn, adapt and develop a different perspective.

And finally, never stop asking why.

Why does the business operate this way? Why are certain decisions being made? What could be improved? How can finance contribute beyond producing another set of financial statements?

Moving from the Big Four to industry was never about leaving behind what I had learned. It was about building on that foundation and learning to see the bigger picture.

Looking back, I am grateful for the five years in audit that shaped my professional foundation, the people who made the journey memorable, and the opportunities that pushed me beyond what I was initially comfortable with.

I still have plenty to learn, and perhaps that is the most exciting part of the journey.

The past created us, but how we use what we have learned from the past is what allows us to create the future.
